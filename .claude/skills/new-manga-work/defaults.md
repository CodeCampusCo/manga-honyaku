# Thai manga defaults

What a Thai edition of a Japanese comic decides the same way in every work.
These override what is already known about Thai.

## Pronouns are re-chosen, not translated

**Pick the Thai pronoun the character would have in a Thai edition of a Japanese
comic** — not the one that matches the Japanese word, and not the one the
situation would call for if the scene were Thai.

The rows are personas, not people. Take the row that fits each person as their
base; where no row fits, write the persona yourself, from how that kind of
character should sound. Once chosen, the base is fixed — a moment's escalation
is not a change of base — and a base that does move goes to `characters.md`.

The columns are **distance and power, not affection**.

What the grid cannot show:

- **`หนู` is never a second person, `เรา` never a first.**
- **A period character never takes `ผม`, `ฉัน`, `ดิฉัน` or `เธอ`, in any
  column.** `แก` is also what is said to an enemy.
- **Speaks only when needed drops the first person wherever Thai lets a
  sentence stand without one**, and takes their own type's row where it cannot.
- **A boyish woman never takes a man's particle.** `ฮะ` and `ครับ` are not her
  substitute for `ค่ะ`, whatever a Thai speaker of the type would really say.
  Outside the polite column she ends bare and the bluntness is in the wording:
  the softness is gone, the hardness was never added.
- **Escalate the register; do not start at the ceiling.** `ฉัน` while he is still
  arguing, `กู` only in the panel where he stops arguing.

## Honorifics carry the same information — read them

| Japanese | Thai | How |
| --- | --- | --- |
| さん | **คุณ** | Never `ซัง`. |
| ちゃん | **จัง** — transliterate | Adult to a girl, and to boys too. Never `หนู`. |
| くん | **คุง** — transliterate | Adult to a child; a senior to a junior they are close to — a student, a junior at work. |
| 様 | **ท่าน** | Never transliterate. |
| 先生 | **อาจารย์**, **หมอ**, **ทนาย** — from context | Any credentialed expert, not only a schoolteacher; `อาจารย์` covers a mangaka. Read the panel before choosing. |
| 先輩 / 後輩 | **พี่** / **น้อง** | Kinship words, used as pronouns. Never a literal translation. |

**A bare name stays bare.** Where the Japanese drops the suffix, so does the
Thai: `忍って` is the name as a vocative, with nothing in front of it.

**A suffix on a role rather than a name takes the Thai kinship or title form,
never a transliteration** — `バイト君` is `น้องพาร์ทไทม์`, `店長さん` is
`คุณผู้จัดการร้าน`.

## Why an accurate line still reads stiff

**Carry the original's structure and word order across as far as it holds, and
the moment the Thai sounds ขัดหู, rebuild the sentence as Thai** — abandon the
structure, not the wording.

**A noun in Japanese is usually a verb in Thai.** When a Japanese noun is doing
the work of a verb, translate it as a verb. `コーティング量がズバ抜けてる` came
out `ปริมาณที่เคลือบมามันเหนือชั้นกว่าใครเพื่อน`, built to the Japanese's shape;
Thai says it `เคลือบมาเยอะกว่าเจ้าอื่นเยอะเลย`.

**Japanese sound and manner words take an adverb or a verb first**, a noun last.
Leaving one untranslated is normal.

**The final particle is information, not decoration.** Japanese marks stance on
the sentence end — `よ ね ぞ わ の さ ぜ` — and Thai has its own system in the same
slot: stance (`นะ` `สิ` `ล่ะ` `หรอก` `เลย` `ไง` `แหละ` `เนี่ย` `ว่ะ`) and
politeness (`ค่ะ` `ครับ`). The vowel's length carries the feeling: **short is
curt or serious** — `ซิ` `นะ` `เถอะ` `ละ` — **long is coaxing** — `ซี่` `น่า`
`น้า`.

`小口が落ちたぞ!!` came out `โคงุจิตกลงไปแล้ว!!`, which drops `ぞ`.

**Most dialogue lines end with a particle.** The usual exceptions are an
exclamation, a fragment running into the next balloon, and a particle that would
be doing no work.

**Aspect and reason markers go the same way.** `まざっちゃうから...` became
`มันจะปนกันค่ะ…`, which keeps the fact and loses both the `ちゃう` that says *and
that would be a shame* and the `から` that says *that's why*.

**An idiom translated by its parts inverts.** `覚悟を決める` is *to steel
yourself* and came out `ทำใจ`, *to resign yourself* — the opposite stance.

## Politeness lands once per utterance

One `ค่ะ` / `ครับ` per utterance, on the last bubble, however many bubbles the
sentence runs across.

Who gets one at all follows the same distance the pronouns do. A woman speaking
politely ends with `ค่ะ`. **Men among themselves take no particle**: `ครับ`
between peers reads as sarcasm, and is for speaking to someone above them.
`はい` answering a question is itself `ค่ะ` or `ครับ`, and does not count as the
sentence-final particle.

**A pronoun split across bubbles is not repeated**: `私 / 頑張ります!` is `ฉัน`
then `จะตั้งใจทำให้เต็มที่ค่ะ!`, not `ฉัน` then `ฉันจะ…`.

**`นะ` takes `คะ`, `น่ะ` takes `ค่ะ`.** No tone mark on both, or a mái èk on
both:

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **`นะคะ`** | correct |
| **`น่ะค่ะ`** | correct |
| `นะค่ะ` | **wrong** |
| `น่ะคะ` | **wrong** |

Where the particle is doing no work, drop it and let the bare `ค่ะ` or `คะ`
carry the politeness on its own.

## Thai lengthens by opening the vowel

A syllable held is a syllable whose vowel opens: `ちゃ〜ん` is `จางงง`, where the
`ั` of `จัง` becomes `า` and the final consonant runs on.

A `♥` or a `♪` closing a line is a tone marker: render it in the wording and the
drawn-out vowel, **never through a particle the character would not otherwise
use.**

`◯◯◯`, a word withheld, becomes `OOO`. `【】` becomes `[]`.

## Conventions that hold across works

- **`…` at the end of a line is a character trailing off,** not an ellipsis of
  omitted words. Thai keeps the `…` and lets the sentence stay unfinished.
- **Keep the question mark wherever the Japanese has one.** A question the
  Japanese wrote without one gets none.
- **Text drawn on a slant is lettered level.**
